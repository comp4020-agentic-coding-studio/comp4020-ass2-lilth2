Freya Bishop joins for this lecture to make the argument the whole semester
has been circling without stating outright: a suggestion is never neutral,
because someone chose what to suggest, and that choice usually serves the
suggester at least as much as the person receiving it. A pre-filled tip
amount, a completed form field defaulting to opted-in, a smart reply that
happens to keep a conversation — and an engagement metric — going a
little longer: these are all technically autocomplete, in the sense this
course has used the word all semester, and all of them are also, mechanism
for mechanism, a persuasion technique with the persuasion made to look like
a convenience.

The lecture works backward through the semester rather than introducing new
material: the keypad's dictionary had opinions about which words were
common enough to rank first, and whoever built the dictionary made that
call. The feed's recommendations optimised for engagement, not for your
stated interests. The forecast interface hid its uncertainty because
confidence sells better than honesty. None of these were built by someone
twirling a moustache — most were built by people trying to make a good
product — but "consent by autocomplete" names what happens when the easiest
path through an interface is also the one that serves someone else's
interest, dressed as a suggestion rather than a demand.
