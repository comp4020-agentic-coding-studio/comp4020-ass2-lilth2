A writer using a predictive tool — accepting some suggestions, rejecting
others, occasionally taking a suggestion and rewriting it into something
else entirely — produces a text that doesn't have a clean answer to "who
wrote this." Not "the human, obviously," because some sentences are the
model's own words, accepted verbatim. Not "the model," because the model
never decided to write a story about this particular subject, in this
particular voice, and would have produced something else entirely for a
different prompt. The honest answer is somewhere in the editing history,
which almost never gets published alongside the finished piece.

This week takes co-writing tools seriously as a genuinely new position for
a predictive system to occupy — not correcting a typo, not finishing a
search query, but sitting inside the compositional process of something
meant to be read as a considered piece of writing. That raises questions
the earlier weeks didn't need to ask: does accepting a good suggestion
count as writing, in the sense the word usually carries? Does it matter,
if the finished piece is good? The lecture doesn't resolve this — nobody
has — but it gives you enough vocabulary to have the argument precisely
instead of vaguely.

## Outline

- three co-writing tools and how differently each one inserts itself into
  the writing process
- acceptance rate as a measure of who's actually doing the writing
- the disclosure question: does a reader have a right to know which
  sentences were suggested
- what the lab's paired-exchange exercise asks you to notice about your own
  editing choices
