Before a phone had a screen worth looking at, it had twelve keys and twenty-six
letters to fit onto them, and every letter after the first press of a key was
a guess about which word you meant. T9 — "text on 9 keys" — turned that guess
into a dictionary lookup: press the keys for the letters, and the phone
picks the most likely word those keys could spell. Multi-tap, its clumsier
predecessor, made you press a key repeatedly to cycle through its letters.
Both are predictive text. Neither looks like what the term calls to mind now.

That's this week's argument: "autocomplete" and "predictive text" name a
family, not a single interface, and the family is older and stranger than
the autocomplete you use today. The keypad is where the family's central
tension shows up in its plainest form — a small, fixed vocabulary of
possible inputs, mapped onto an ambiguous signal, resolved by a dictionary
that has opinions about what you probably meant. Every later week in this
course is a variation on that tension: what the vocabulary is, what the
signal is, and whose dictionary gets to decide.
